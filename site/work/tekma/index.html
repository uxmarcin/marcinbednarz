Case study, Tekma, web app, designed end to end

A hiring app where the decision rests on what the candidate actually said

Tekma is a hiring app. Every candidate gets the same questions from a rubric, two interviewers score independently before anyone sees the other score, an AI notetaker keeps the transcript, and the work does not end at the offer: the new person gets a 90-day plan with 1:1s and check-ins. I designed it on my own from start to finish: the information architecture, every flow and screen in Polish and English, the components, the copy and the landing page.

The problem

Interviews decided on impressions, notes lost in notebooks

In most small and mid-sized companies hiring runs on gut feeling. Each interviewer asks different questions, notes end up in a notebook or a chat, and the person with the strongest opinion in the room decides. Candidates wait weeks for an answer. After the offer, nobody owns the first months of the new hire.

The design goal was to make the fair way the easy way: the same questions for everyone, scores you can compare, an answer for every candidate, and a plan after the offer.

The dashboard

What needs attention today, not a wall of numbers

The dashboard opens with the things a hiring manager has to act on: upcoming interviews, a rating gap between two interviewers, an overdue 1:1, a candidate waiting for a GDPR reply. Open jobs sit below as a pipeline from application to offer. The sidebar follows the two halves of the product: hiring and after hiring.

Rubrics and scorecards

The same questions for everyone, two scores before a decision

A rubric is the backbone of a role: the questions, what a good answer covers, and the weight of each question. The rubric editor shows the whole interview on one page, so the hiring manager can see if the questions actually test what the job needs.

After the interview each interviewer fills in a scorecard on their own. The second score stays hidden until both are in. When the two scores differ by a lot, the app flags the gap and asks the two people to talk before the decision. The comparison view then puts candidates side by side, question by question.

AI in the interview

AI suggests, a person always decides

During the interview an AI notetaker writes the transcript, so the interviewer can listen instead of typing. A live coach on the side shows which parts of the rubric the candidate has not touched yet and suggests a follow-up question. Only the interviewer sees the hints, and the candidate agrees to the recording before the call.

Before the interview, the coach prepares the interviewer: the candidate's background, the gaps in their application and the questions worth asking. After it, the notetaker turns the transcript into notes tied to the rubric. The score is always typed in by a person.

Pipeline and process

Who is stuck where, and who should move on

The pipeline shows every candidate by stage with the number of days they have been waiting. The rule in the product is simple: every candidate gets an answer within ten days. The intake form with the hiring manager starts the role, and the decision log keeps a record of who decided what and why, so the team can come back to it months later.

Messaging keeps the conversation with each candidate in one thread, with templates for the messages people put off, like a rejection. The talent pool holds the people worth coming back to for the next role.

GDPR and the EU AI Act

Compliance built into the screens, not into a PDF

A hiring tool with AI in Europe has to answer two questions for every candidate: what data do we hold, and where did AI take part. Tekma keeps data and recordings in the EU and shows it. The GDPR and AI Act panel lists consents, retention dates and data requests with their deadlines. The privacy screen lets a candidate's data be exported or deleted in one place.

Job posts get a compliance check before they go out: pay range, required wording, nothing that discriminates. The post then comes out in several graphic styles and formats, ready to publish.

The candidate side

The candidate always knows where they stand

The candidate portal works on a phone, because that is where people check it. It shows the stage, the next step and the date, the people the candidate will meet, and the consents they gave. A candidate can ask for their data or withdraw a consent from the same screen. No login wall, one link from the message.

After the offer

The offer is not the end: a 90-day plan, 1:1s and check-ins

Most hiring tools stop at the signed offer. In Tekma the person moves from the pipeline to People, with a 90-day plan: goals for the first weeks, 1:1s with the manager, and short mood check-ins. The dashboard warns when a 1:1 is overdue or a check-in drops. The idea is that the first months decide whether the hire worked, so they should be as visible as the interviews were.

Process

From the information architecture to the landing page

I started with the information architecture and the two halves of the product, hiring and after hiring, then the flows: intake, rubric, interview, scorecard, decision, offer, 90-day plan. Then the screens in Figma, in Polish and English, with a component library and the states for empty, loading and error. I wrote the product copy and the requirements for engineering. The landing page uses the same tokens as the app, so the marketing site and the product feel like one thing.

The design has the empty states, the errors and the edge cases from the start, because every state skipped in Figma comes back as a question from engineering.

Where it stands

Early access

Tekma is in early access at tekma.app. The screens on this page come from the product design. There are no usage numbers to show yet.

For me, success would mean that a team of 20 people runs its next hire with the same questions for everyone, two scores before the decision, and an answer to every candidate within ten days.

What I learned

The fair way has to be the easy way

Nobody wants to hire unfairly. People skip the rubric because it takes time. So the product does the tedious part: the questions are there, the notes write themselves, the second score is hidden until both are in. The fair process became the path of least resistance.

Next, I would watch the first hires in early access and see where people leave the rubric. That is where the design needs work.
